Pay per appointment vs a monthly retainer

The payment model tells you how the bill is calculated. The qualification rules tell you whether the appointment is worth buying.

Pay-per-appointment pricing makes part of the bill depend on booked meetings. A monthly retainer charges a fixed service fee for the agreed work. Either model needs a clear scope, a useful qualification standard and terms you understand.

Our model is a combination: $100 per qualified booked appointment, plus $1,500 per month for infrastructure. At 20 appointments, the monthly total is $3,500. Appointment-based pricing does not mean there are no fixed costs.

Compare what triggers the invoice

Start with the billable event. One provider may charge when an appointment is booked. Another may use attendance or a different qualification test. Some providers offer several models; ConnectLead's service page is one published example. A label such as pay per meeting is not enough to tell you what you are buying.

Our stated unit is a qualified booked appointment. The prospect must be a decision-maker inside your ideal customer profile and must have agreed to hear the offer.

Before signing, ask how the provider treats the cases that sit around that definition. What happens if the person cancels, the company is outside the agreed market, or the booking turns out to be with someone who cannot make the decision? Ask for the actual terms rather than assuming a particular replacement or credit policy.

This is a comparison framework, not a description of every agency contract. There is no automatic quality advantage in the pricing label. A provider can book poor meetings under either model.

Calculate the same volume before comparing price

For our model, the calculation is straightforward:

20 appointments x $100 + $1,500 infrastructure = $3,500.

That is $175 per qualified booked appointment when the infrastructure charge is included. At 30 appointments, the total is $4,500 and the effective cost is $150 each.

When comparing that with a retainer proposal, use the same scope and appointment definition. Include any build fee, data costs, sending tools and minimum commitment described in the quote. A lower monthly number can cover a different job.

Keep replies, bookings and held meetings separate

An interested reply is a reason to continue the conversation. It is not yet a sales call. A booking is not proof that the call happened. A held meeting is not proof of a closed customer.

The Networked fundraising case study reports 188 meetings booked and 132 held over its campaign window. Those are different stages with different counts. This is one fundraising engagement, not an attendance benchmark for a B2B sales campaign.

Ask a provider to show its definitions alongside the headline result. If one report counts positive replies and another counts held meetings, the two totals cannot be compared as though they describe the same outcome.

Match the commitment to the way you buy

A fixed service fee gives you a known service charge for the period. Appointment-based pricing gives you a bill that changes with its defined appointment count. Hybrid models add a fixed component to that variable charge.

For any of them, ask about the minimum commitment, notice period and which work continues between booked meetings. Our offer starts rolling monthly, with no long contract. We own strategy, data, copy, infrastructure, email and LinkedIn outreach, reply handling and booking. You show up and close.

Our guarantee is 20 qualified meetings. 30 days. Or we work for free. The 30-day period starts when campaigns go live. Agree the qualification criteria and written remedy before launch; the pricing label alone does not explain them.

Choose the proposal you can inspect

You should be able to name the buyer, explain what makes a meeting qualified and calculate the complete bill. You should also know who handles the work between the first reply and the booked call.
